The short answer

Before 1858, wills were proved by church courts. The National Archives holds the wills proved by the most senior of these, the Prerogative Court of Canterbury (PCC).

More than a million PCC wills are in the catalogue series PROB 11. You can search them by name and download a copy.

{{ tnaDetails({ title: 'What "proved" means', text: 'A will was proved when a court accepted it as valid and gave the executors authority to carry it out. The date a will was proved is usually later than the date it was written.' }) }}

What you need before you start

  • the person's name, including other spellings
  • roughly when they died
  • where they lived or owned property
{{ tnaDetails({ title: 'Why where they lived matters', body: '

The PCC covered the south of England and Wales, and people with property in more than one area. Wealthier people were more likely to use it.

If the person lived in the north of England, or left little property, their will may have been proved in a local court. Those records are held by local archives.

' }) }}

How to search

  1. Search the catalogue for the person's name.
  2. Filter the results to the collection PROB.
  3. Narrow the dates to a few years after the person died.
  4. Open a result to check the name, place and occupation.
{{ tnaDetails({ title: 'Tips if you get too many or too few results', body: '
  • Try other spellings. Clerks wrote names as they heard them.
  • Search by surname and place if the first name is common.
  • Widen the dates. A will could be proved years after a death.
' }) }}

See an example record

The will of William Shakespeare was proved in the PCC in June 1616. Open its catalogue record to see what a will record looks like and how to view it.

If you cannot find a will

Most people before 1858 did not leave a will. If there is no PCC will, the next places to look are:

  • records of administrations, made when someone died without a will
  • local church court records, held by local archives
  • death duty records, from 1796
